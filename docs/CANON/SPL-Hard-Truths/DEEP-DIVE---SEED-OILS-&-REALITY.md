# DEEP DIVE - SEED OILS & REALITY

_Working position based on current evidence + known caveats_

Seed oils have been turned into a cartoon villain.

One side: “toxic industrial sludge.”\
Other side: “heart-healthy miracle, don’t ask questions.”

SPL lives in the middle: physiology, data, and common sense. Not vibes, not panic.

This is the canon version for SPL: what seed oils actually are, what the science says, where it’s genuinely nuanced, and how to act like an adult around fryers.

---

# 1. WHAT ARE SEED OILS?

When people say “seed oils,” they’re usually talking about high-linoleic vegetable oils:

- soybean  
- corn  
- sunflower  
- safflower  
- cottonseed  
- grapeseed  
- generic “vegetable oil” blends

Most mass-market versions are **refined, bleached, deodorised (RBD)**:

1. Seeds are cleaned and ground.  
1. Oil is extracted, often using **hexane** as a solvent (very efficient for pulling oil out of crushed seeds).  
1. The mixture is heated so hexane evaporates and is recovered.  
1. The crude oil is degummed, neutralised, bleached, and deodorised to remove odours and off-flavours.

Under EU law (Directive 2009/32/EC), residual hexane in fats and oils is capped at **1 mg/kg**, and monitoring data show typical commercial oils below that limit. Risk reviews from EFSA and others consider consumer exposure at those levels to be well below toxicological concern.

**SPL position:**\
Hexane is part of the industrial story, but at regulated residues it’s not the main health threat. We _prefer_ simpler, minimally processed fats when accessible, because they usually co-travel with better diet patterns and fewer unknowns — but we don’t build our worldview around solvent panic.

---

# 2. OMEGA-6, LDL & HEART RISK — MAJORITY VIEW VS MINORITY VIEW

Most serious data look at **what happens when you replace saturated fat with omega-6–rich polyunsaturated fats (PUFAs)**.

Broad strokes:

- The AHA advisory and related work: higher omega-6 (mainly linoleic acid) **lowers LDL-C** when it replaces saturated fat, and is associated with **equal or lower cardiovascular risk**, not higher.  
- Prospective cohorts generally find higher dietary or circulating linoleic acid linked to **lower CHD and all-cause mortality**, especially when part of heart-healthy patterns.

But it’s not as simple as “more omega-6 = fewer heart attacks forever.”

The 2018 **Cochrane review** on omega-6 fats found:

- Increasing omega-6 “may make little or no difference” to deaths or total cardiovascular events.  
- It **may reduce myocardial infarction (heart attack) risk**, but the evidence is low quality and heterogenous.

Some older trials (e.g. Sydney Diet Heart Study, parts of Minnesota Coronary Experiment) suggest:

- Linoleic acid lowered cholesterol.  
- Event outcomes were mixed and not consistently better — in some re-analyses, mortality was worse in the high-LA arms.

Important context: those interventions often used **margarines that contained trans fats**, which on their own raise CHD risk.

**SPL bottom line:**

- Majority view (AHA, etc.): replacing some saturated fat with omega-6 PUFAs is **neutral to modestly beneficial** for CHD risk, especially as part of an overall heart-healthy diet.  
- Minority view: dropping LDL purely by cranking linoleic acid isn’t necessarily identical, event-wise, to dropping LDL via statins + MONO-heavy diets + exercise. Mechanism matters.

We align with the majority, but we hold space for “not all LDL-lowering strategies are identical.”

# 3. INFLAMMATION, FADS GENES & INDIVIDUAL VARIATION

On average, human studies do **not** support the meme that normal omega-6 intakes make everyone more inflamed:

- Meta-analyses and trials typically show no increase in systemic inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α) at typical linoleic acid intakes; some show neutral or small favourable shifts.

But there’s a real genetic wrinkle: **FADS1 and FADS2**.

- FADS1/2 encode Δ-5 and Δ-6 desaturases, which convert linoleic acid (LA, 18:2) into arachidonic acid (AA, 20:4).  
- Common FADS SNPs alter desaturase activity and therefore **AA:LA ratio** in membranes and plasma.

Key findings:

- Certain FADS haplotypes are associated with **higher AA:LA**, higher CRP, and higher odds of coronary artery disease.  
- These genotypes effectively make some people _better AA factories_ from the same linoleic acid intake.

**Implication for SPL:**

- Population-level: “omega-6 = inflammation” is not supported.  
- Individual-level: if you’re a high-AA, high-CRP, high-risk FADS carrier eating a very high omega-6 / very low omega-3 Western diet, your personal risk profile might be different from the average.

We already care about **individual data**: ApoB, AA:LA ratio, hs-CRP, genetics where available. FADS status is one of several reasons why “it depends” is not a cop-out.

[Note: For people of West African descent, the risk of inflammation from high seed oil intake is statistically higher than for East Asians.](https://slite.com/api/public/notes/WPFk2H3Sm_tnfK/redirect)

# 4. DEEP FRYING, REUSED OIL & WHY VATS ARE A DIFFERENT UNIVERSE

Now to the part where seed oils really can get nasty: **high-temperature, repeated deep frying**.

When PUFA-rich oils are held at 170–190°C and reused again and again:

- **Oxidation**  
    - PUFAs react with oxygen → primary peroxides → secondary products (aldehydes like 4-HNE, acrolein, etc.).  
    - These aldehydes are reactive and can damage proteins, lipids, and DNA in experimental models.  
- **Polymerisation & polar compounds**  
    - The oil accumulates oxidised triglycerides, diglycerides, free fatty acids and other **polar compounds**.  
    - Total polar compounds (TPC) are standard markers of “how abused” a frying oil is.  
- **Trans and cyclic fats**  
    - Long, hot frying promotes the formation of trans isomers and other degraded lipid structures.

Regulators respond by setting **discard limits**:

- Many standards and regulations (e.g. AOCS guidance, FSSAI RUCO programme) treat ~**25% TPC** as the maximum: above that, the oil is considered unfit for human consumption.

Epidemiology isn’t perfect, but:

- Heavy consumption of deep-fried foods is repeatedly associated with higher cardiometabolic risk, in part via these oxidised oil products plus the ultra-processed food matrix they come with.

**SPL translation:**

> The real problem isn’t “seed oil touched my food.”\
> The problem is **seed oil that has been thrashed all day in a commercial fryer and never thrown out**, wrapped around ultra-processed starch, salt and meat, eaten often.

We don’t fear a tablespoon of oil in a pan. We absolutely respect the biology of “cheap deep-fried crispy everything, all the time.”

# 5. SMOKE POINT VS OXIDATIVE STABILITY — WHY HOME HIGH HEAT STILL MATTERS

This is a key nuance: **smoke point ≠ oxidative stability**.

- Many refined seed oils have **high smoke points** on paper.  
- But they’re high in PUFAs, which are more chemically fragile at high heat.

A 2018 experimental study (Guillaume et al.) heated multiple commercial oils and measured actual oxidation products:

- Smoke point was a **poor predictor** of oxidative stability.  
- Oils richer in monounsaturated fat and antioxidants (like extra virgin olive oil) produced _fewer_ polar compounds and oxidative breakdown products than some refined seed oils, despite lower labelled smoke points.

Multiple lines of work now point the same way:

- **EVOO and high-oleic oils** hold up surprisingly well at typical cooking temps.  
- PUFA-heavy oils generate more oxidation products at the same heat.

**Practical consequence:**

> Even in home cooking, if you’re repeatedly searing at very high heat with a high-PUFA oil, you are generating more aldehydes than if you used EVOO, avocado oil, high-oleic oils, or saturated fats — _even if you never see visible smoke_.

So home use is not automatically “chemically benign.” It’s just less extreme than industrial fryers.

# 6. OIL TYPES THROUGH AN SPL LENS

Forget “good vs evil.” Think **job**, **composition**, **processing**, and **pattern**.

## 6.1 Saturated-heavy fats

**Butter, ghee, tallow, lard, palm, coconut**

- Pros:  
    - very heat-stable  
    - low oxidation at frying temps  
- Cons:  
    - raise LDL-C and ApoB in many people, especially at higher intakes, increasing atherosclerotic risk.

**SPL use:**\
Brilliant for flavour and high-heat searing in **moderate amounts**, especially if your ApoB is under control. Not ideal as the primary calorie base for someone with high cardiovascular risk.

---

## 6.2 Monounsaturated-dominant oils

**Extra virgin olive oil, avocado oil, high-oleic sunflower/canola, refined olive oil**

- Strong human data for EVOO in Mediterranean-style diets: lower CVD and mortality.  
- High monounsaturates + antioxidants → **excellent oxidative stability**, even at higher cooking temps.

**SPL use:**\
These are the **default workhorse oils** in the SPL universe.

---

## 6.3 Standard high-linoleic seed oils

**Regular sunflower, safflower, soybean, corn, cottonseed, generic “vegetable oil”**

- Pros:  
    - low in saturated fat  
    - lower LDL vs saturated fat when used as a replacement in otherwise decent diets  
- Cons:  
    - more prone to oxidation at high and repeated heat  
    - main fat used in ultra-processed and deep-fried foods

**SPL use (nuanced):**

- **Low–medium heat home cooking (no reuse):** generally fine for most people inside a mostly whole-food diet.  
- **High-heat searing / wok-hei / repeated roasting:** chemically inferior to high-oleic or saturated options; better to use EVOO, avocado, high-oleic oils, ghee, or tallow here.  
- **Industrial vats and fast-food fryers:** part of a clearly worse pattern; treat as “sometimes” food, not a staple.

---

## 6.4 Cold-pressed vs refined

- **Cold-pressed / minimally processed:** fewer industrial steps, retain more phenolics/tocopherols, typically better flavour and sometimes better oxidative behaviour.  
- **Refined:** stripped of most minor compounds, more neutral and shelf-stable.

All else equal, SPL leans toward **cold-pressed and minimally processed**, but recognises cost and availability as real constraints.

---

# 7. HEXANE & “INDUSTRIALNESS”

Hexane extraction sounds sinister. The boring regulatory reality:

- EU Directive 2009/32/EC sets maximum hexane residues at **1 mg/kg** in fats and oils; monitoring data show typical oils below this.  
- Reviews from EFSA, EUFIC, and independent labs conclude that consumer exposure via refined oils is **far below levels of toxicological concern**.

**SPL stance:**

- Hexane residues at regulated levels are **unlikely to be a meaningful health driver**.  
- We still favour fats that don’t require heavy solvent-based extraction when realistic, because simplicity is a decent long-term heuristic.

Risk lives more in **how** the oil is used and **what foods** it lives in than in the mere presence of hexane in its origin story.

---

# 8. WHAT’S STILL UNCERTAIN

Three honest open questions:

1. **Chronic oxidised oil exposure at real-world doses**

    - Mechanistic and animal data say aldehydes and oxidised lipids can be nasty.  
    - Long-term human trials at typical intake levels are hard to run, so we infer from patterns and biomarkers, not perfect RCTs.

1. **Very high omega-6 + low omega-3 + risky FADS genotypes**

    - People with high-activity FADS variants may convert more LA to AA and run higher pro-inflammatory tone on the same diet.  
    - We don’t yet have crisp population-level rules for “safe upper limits” for those individuals, beyond the obvious: don’t build your diet on junk, and get enough omega-3.

1. **Equivalence of omega-6 vs other LDL-lowering strategies**

    - Cochrane and re-analyses highlight uncertainty about whether raising omega-6 _alone_ delivers the same event protection as other LDL-lowering interventions.

For SPL, this doesn’t invalidate seed oils. It just tells us not to treat “swap butter for sunflower oil” as salvation.

# 9. SPL PRACTICAL POSITION

Here’s how this all collapses into behaviour.

1. **Seed oils are not magical poison.**\
At realistic intakes, inside a mostly whole-food diet, and especially when they displace some saturated fat and refined carbs, omega-6–rich oils are **neutral to modestly beneficial** for cardiovascular risk at the population level.  
1. **Home cooking still needs heat awareness.**

    - Low–moderate heat: using refined seed oils occasionally is unlikely to be a big issue.  
    - Very high heat and repeated roasting/searing: upgrade to EVOO, avocado, high-oleic oils, ghee, or tallow — not just for flavour, but for chemistry.

1. **Industrial deep-fried food is the real villain.**

    - Reused PUFA-rich oils, TPC near or beyond regulatory cut-offs, plus ultra-processed food matrices and high frequency of intake — that’s where we see consistent risk signals.

1. **Genetics and omega-3 status matter.**

    - If your data show high AA:LA, high CRP, unfriendly FADS variants and a junk-heavy diet, that’s a nudge to:  
        - push omega-3 intake up (fish, algae oils)  
        - stop bathing in ultra-processed seed-oil junk  
        - keep omega-6 in sane, not extreme, ranges

1. **A simple SPL fat hierarchy for real life**

    - **Default workhorses**  
        - extra virgin olive oil  
        - avocado oil  
        - high-oleic canola/sunflower  
    - **Support cast**  
        - standard refined seed oils for low–medium heat, non-reused  
        - butter, ghee, tallow, coconut for flavour and high-heat jobs in modest amounts, watching ApoB  
    - **Limit as a pattern**  
        - fast-food fries, industrial deep-fried snacks, and anything clearly coming out of oil that should have been binned yesterday

1. **Context beats ingredients.**\
ApoB, TG:HDL, VO₂max, muscle mass, sleep, body composition and ultra-processed load tell you far more about your future than the fact you used sunflower oil to sauté onions last night.

# Additional Notes

## FADS Genes

FADS1 and FADS2 gene variants affect how people process seed oils. It states "individual-level risk." However, the science shows this is not just individual—it is strongly stratified by ancestry due to evolutionary adaptation.

The Scientific Detail: The FADS gene cluster regulates the enzymes that convert plant Linoleic Acid (LA) into Arachidonic Acid (AA), which drives inflammation.

- Ancestral Context: Populations that historically had limited access to dietary fats evolved to be "efficient converters" (to prevent deficiency). Populations with high-fat diets (like the Inuit) evolved to be "slow converters" (to prevent toxicity).  
- The Data:  
    - African Ancestry: Approximately 80% of African Americans carry the "efficient converter" alleles (rs174537). This means they convert seed oils to inflammatory Arachidonic Acid much more rapidly than other groups. In the context of a modern high-omega-6 diet, this creates a higher "inflammatory load" from the same bowl of food compared to someone of European descent.  
    - European Ancestry: Approximately 45% carry the efficient converter alleles.  
    - East Asian Ancestry: Approximately 30% carry the efficient converter alleles.  
    - Inuit/Greenlandic: Nearly 98% carry the "slow converter" alleles (blocking the conversion).

**So it depends on your genes" is true, but for people of West African descent, the risk of inflammation from high seed oil intake is statistically higher than for East Asians.**
