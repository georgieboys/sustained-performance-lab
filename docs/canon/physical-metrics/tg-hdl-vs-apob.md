**Two Complementary Markers for Structural Risk and Metabolic State**

Sustained Performance Lab evaluates cardiometabolic health through two primary lipid markers: **Apolipoprotein B (ApoB)** and the **triglyceride-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (TG:HDL)**. Each marker reflects a different layer of physiology. ApoB quantifies atherogenic particle burden; TG:HDL reflects the metabolic environment in which those particles are produced, remodeled, and cleared.

Both markers matter.\
They answer different questions about long-term function.\
SPL tracks both but uses the TG:HDL ratio as the **primary metabolic state marker**, and ApoB as the **primary structural risk marker**.

---

## 1. ApoB as a Structural Risk Marker

ApoB is the structural protein carried by all atherogenic lipoproteins: LDL, VLDL, IDL, and Lp(a). Since each particle contains exactly one ApoB molecule, a plasma ApoB measurement provides a direct count of circulating particles capable of entering the arterial wall and contributing to atherosclerotic plaque.

Higher ApoB indicates:

- greater cumulative arterial exposure  
- increased likelihood of lipid penetration into the endothelium  
- higher long-term risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease

ApoB is one of the most consistent lipid predictors of cardiovascular events across diverse populations.

What ApoB does _not_ reveal is the metabolic state that produced those particles. It is a structural outcome measure, not a systems-level description.

---

## 2. The TG:HDL Ratio as a Metabolic State Marker

The TG:HDL ratio compresses complex metabolic phenomena into a single measurement. It reflects the interplay between liver function, lipoprotein transport, fat oxidation, and insulin signaling.

A higher TG:HDL ratio is associated with:

- hepatic insulin resistance  
- increased VLDL production  
- impaired triglyceride clearance  
- reduced HDL-mediated reverse cholesterol transport  
- formation of small, dense LDL particles  
- increased risk of NAFLD  
- reduced metabolic flexibility

The ratio often identifies metabolic drift earlier than glucose, HbA1c, or fasting insulin in otherwise healthy individuals. It is responsive to sleep, diet composition, aerobic conditioning, alcohol intake, body composition, and mitochondrial efficiency.

---

## 3. What TG:HDL Reveals That ApoB Does Not

Although both markers relate to lipid metabolism, the TG:HDL ratio provides upstream information about the system in which atherogenic particles are produced.

### Hepatic Insulin Resistance

When the liver becomes insulin resistant, it continues to release glucose and increases VLDL production. This elevates triglycerides and depresses HDL through increased lipid exchange and particle remodeling.

### Lipoprotein Quality

High TG:HDL ratios correlate with the presence of small, dense LDL particles—structurally more atherogenic despite identical ApoB counts.

### Reverse Cholesterol Transport Efficiency

Reduced HDL capacity and faster clearance of smaller HDL particles reflect impaired cholesterol shuttling back to the liver.

### Fat Oxidation and Mitochondrial Function

A high ratio often corresponds to impaired triglyceride clearance and reduced mitochondrial efficiency.

### Early Metabolic Drift

The ratio may reveal unfavorable metabolic trends before glucose or insulin-based markers cross diagnostic thresholds.

ApoB alone does not identify these upstream phenomena.

---

## 4. What ApoB Reveals That TG:HDL Cannot

ApoB remains essential because it quantifies the particle burden itself. Even with a favorable TG:HDL ratio, ApoB may be elevated due to:

- genetically higher LDL production  
- variations in LDL receptor activity  
- elevated Lp(a)  
- baseline lipoprotein kinetics independent of metabolic dysfunction

The TG:HDL ratio provides context; ApoB provides particle count.\
The two measurements answer different questions and are interpreted together.

---

## 5. Pattern Interpretation Within SPL

Four patterns recur across assessments:

### High ApoB + High TG:HDL

Structural risk and metabolic dysfunction are both present. This is the highest-risk quadrant.

### High ApoB + Low TG:HDL

Metabolic machinery is functioning well, but particle number is elevated—often a genetic phenotype. Structural risk remains.

### Normal ApoB + High TG:HDL

Metabolic dysfunction is emerging despite normal particle count. Patterns consistent with early insulin resistance, NAFLD risk, and altered lipoprotein remodeling.

### Low ApoB + Low TG:HDL

Low atherogenic particle burden with a flexible metabolic system. This is the lowest-risk physiologic profile.

---

## 6. Why SPL Uses TG:HDL as the Primary Metabolic Metric

SPL focuses on building durable, long-functioning systems: stable frames, powerful engines, and adaptable metabolism. The TG:HDL ratio aligns with this philosophy because it reflects system behavior, not just structural outcome.

TG:HDL is:

- sensitive to metabolic changes  
- responsive to training and nutritional interventions  
- reflective of liver function and fat oxidation  
- indicative of early metabolic drift  
- tied to insulin sensitivity and mitochondrial efficiency  
- easily obtained from standard lipid panels  
- interpretable in context over time

ApoB is crucial for understanding atherosclerotic exposure, but it does not provide the rapid, dynamic feedback required for SPL’s training and health frameworks.

---

## 7. The SPL Position

Within Sustained Performance Lab:

- **ApoB** is the primary **structural risk marker** for atherosclerotic disease.  
- **TG:HDL** is the primary **metabolic state marker**, reflecting insulin sensitivity, hepatic function, and lipoprotein quality.  
- Neither number is treated as an isolated determinant. Each represents a complementary readout of a shared system.

ApoB describes arterial exposure.\
TG:HDL describes metabolic direction.

The objective of SPL is not solely to minimise structural risk but to maintain metabolic resilience and longevity across decades. Tracking both markers enables a full view of the system that underlies long-term performance.

## REFERENCES

1. **ApoB, particle number & ASCVD risk**

    - Robinson JG et al. “Meta-analysis of comparison of effectiveness of lowering apolipoprotein B versus low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and non–high-density lipoprotein cholesterol for cardiovascular risk reduction.” _Am J Cardiol_ (2012). Shows apoB reduction tracks more closely with CHD, stroke and CVD risk reduction than LDL-C or non-HDL-C.  
    - De Oliveira-Gomes D et al. “Apolipoprotein B: Bridging the Gap Between Evidence and Practice.” _Circulation_ (2024). Reviews apoB as a superior marker of atherogenic particle burden and ASCVD risk versus LDL-C and non-HDL-C.  
    - Participant-level meta-analyses and European prevention papers summarising apoB as the preferred marker for atherogenic lipoproteins and residual risk.

1. **TG:HDL ratio & insulin resistance / metabolic syndrome**

    - Chiang JK et al. “Predicting insulin resistance using the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio in Taiwanese adults.” _Cardiovasc Diabetol_ (2011). Supports TG:HDL as a simple clinical indicator of insulin resistance, with ethnic-specific thresholds.  
    - Iwani NAKZ et al. “Triglyceride to HDL-C Ratio is Associated with Insulin Resistance in Overweight and Obese Children.” _Sci Rep_ (2017). Extends TG:HDL–insulin resistance association to paediatric populations.  
    - Mostafa SA et al. “The Association of the Triglyceride-to-HDL Cholesterol Ratio with Insulin Resistance by Ethnicity.” _PLoS ONE_ (2012). Confirms TG:HDL as an IR marker but highlights ethnic differences in its predictive strength.  
    - Baneu P et al. “The Triglyceride/HDL Ratio as a Surrogate Biomarker for Insulin Resistance: A PRISMA-Based Review.” _Biomedicines_ (2024). Systematic review on TG:HDL as an indirect IR marker and its metabolic underpinnings.

1. **TG:HDL ratio, small dense LDL & lipoprotein quality**

    - Maruyama C et al. “Assessment of LDL particle size by triglyceride/HDL cholesterol ratio.” _Clin Chim Acta_ (2003). Shows higher TG:HDL ratios strongly associated with small, dense LDL.  
    - Bhalodkar NC et al. “Accuracy of the ratio of triglycerides to HDL cholesterol for predicting small, dense LDL.” _Am J Cardiol_ (2006). Proposes TG:HDL cut-offs that track with sdLDL patterns.  
    - Morales Alcazar C. “Measurement of small, dense lipoproteins. Usefulness of the Triglyceride/HDL ratio.” _Eur J Prev Cardiol_ (2023, supplement). Summarises TG:HDL >2 as indicating sdLDL predominance.  
    - Kosmas CE et al. “The Triglyceride/High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) Ratio: A Valuable Marker of Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Risk and Small Dense LDL.” _Diagnostics_ (2023). Reviews TG:HDL as a marker of sdLDL-rich, pro-atherogenic profiles.  
    - Chiriacò M et al. “Proatherogenic changes in lipoprotein particles associated with a high TG/HDL ratio.” _Obesity_ (2023). Links higher TG:HDL to larger VLDL, smaller LDL/HDL particles.

1. **TG:HDL ratio & cardiovascular outcomes / mortality**

    - Bittner V et al. “The TG/HDL Cholesterol Ratio Predicts All-Cause Mortality in Women with Suspected Ischemia.” _J Am Coll Cardiol_ (ANCILLARY/WISE substudy, 2009). Shows TG:HDL as an independent predictor of mortality and events.  
    - Wan K et al. “The Association between Triglyceride/High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Ratio and All-Cause Mortality in Acute Coronary Syndrome after Coronary Revascularization.” _PLoS ONE_ (2015). Demonstrates TG:HDL as a powerful predictor of all-cause mortality in ACS patients post-PCI.  
    - Zhou S et al. “Triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio and major adverse cardiovascular events in ACS patients undergoing PCI.” _Sci Rep_ (2024). Confirms TG:HDL as a robust predictor of MACE, all-cause and cardiac death.  
    - Poochanasri M et al. “Triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein ratio as a predictor for major adverse cardiovascular events.” _J Health Popul Nutr_ (2025). Summarises cohort data linking high TG:HDL with increased MACE risk.  
    - Young KA et al. “Triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio and incident diabetes and coronary disease.” _Metab Syndr Relat Disord_ (2019). Evaluates TG:HDL as a predictor of diabetes and CHD across ethnic groups.  
    - Cassano V et al. “Possible correlation between Triglyceride/HDL ratio and mortality across cardiometabolic conditions.” _Metabolism_ (2025, in press). Suggests elevated TG:HDL associates with mortality in CAD, aortic dissection and T2DM populations.  
    - Triglycerides and TG/HDL ratio analyses in _JAHA_ (2019) and other cohorts, showing predictive value for MACE and residual risk beyond traditional lipids.

1. **Mechanistic and review perspectives**

    - Chauhan A et al. “Triglyceride/HDL ratio: A marker for insulin resistance and cardiovascular disease.” _J Family Med Prim Care_ (2021). Narrative review linking TG:HDL to IR, sdLDL, endothelial dysfunction and CVD.  
    - Cooper Institute summary: “The Blood Triglyceride:HDL Ratio and LDL Particle Size.” Educational overview of TG:HDL as a clinical sdLDL proxy and CHD risk signal.  
    - Kosmas CE et al. (Diagnostics 2023, above) for an integrated perspective on TG:HDL as both metabolic and atherosclerotic risk marker.

1. **Context with apoB and other lipids**

    - Robinson JG et al. meta-analysis (Am J Cardiol 2012, above) plus later participant-level meta-analyses showing apoB as a better structural risk marker than LDL-C/non-HDL-C.  
    - De Oliveira-Gomes D et al. (Circulation 2024, above) for contemporary guidance on integrating apoB with traditional lipids in risk assessment and management.
